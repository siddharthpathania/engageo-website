Someone calls your clinic at 9:40 PM. Your front desk left at 7. The phone rings out, and nothing about that call is recorded anywhere — not the number, not the reason, not the fact that it happened at all.

The next morning your receptionist starts with whoever calls first. The 9:40 PM caller is not in that queue. If they needed an appointment, they have probably already booked one somewhere else.

This is a playbook for that gap: what to do with calls that arrive when nobody is at the desk, what to automate, what to never automate, and how to make the morning handover routine instead of accidental.

## Decide what "after hours" actually means

Most clinics have three different gaps, and they need different handling:

- \*\*Closed hours.\*\* Nights, Sundays, holidays. Nobody is in the building.
- \*\*Open but unavailable.\*\* The desk is staffed, but the receptionist is with a patient, on another line, or handling billing. This is usually the biggest gap and the least visible one.
- \*\*Doctor-dependent hours.\*\* The clinic is open, the doctor is in surgery or consulting, and no one can answer questions that need the doctor.

Write down the actual hours for each. If you skip this, you will end up building one automation for "after hours" that fires at 3 PM on a Tuesday when the desk was simply busy, and the patient will notice the mismatch.

## Know what an after-hours caller wants

Almost every call outside desk hours is one of four things:

1. \*\*Book, reschedule or cancel an appointment.\*\* The largest group, and the one that is fully automatable.
2. \*\*A routine question\*\* about timings, fees, location, documents to bring, or whether a report is ready.
3. \*\*A follow-up concern\*\* after a procedure — pain, swelling, medication doubts.
4. \*\*A genuine emergency.\*\*

The first two can be handled without a human being awake. The third needs a clinician's judgment, and the fourth needs a hospital, not a message.

Your after-hours system should be built to sort calls into these four buckets quickly, and to be conservative: when it cannot tell the difference between the third and the fourth, it must behave as if it is the fourth.

## Capture the call before you do anything clever

Before automation, fix the recording. Every unanswered call should leave a row somewhere your team sees in the morning: the number, the time, whether the caller left a message, and what happened next.

Without that, you cannot tell whether your after-hours handling works. With it, even a manual callback list beats what most clinics do today.

Conditional call forwarding on your existing number is usually enough to start: unanswered calls forward to a second number that records them, rather than ringing into nothing.

## Reply on WhatsApp — and understand the rules before you build

WhatsApp is the natural channel for a missed call in India, but the platform has a specific rule that decides what you can send and when.

When a person messages or calls your business on WhatsApp, a \*\*24-hour customer service window\*\* opens. Inside that window you can send ordinary free-form messages; the timer resets each time the user messages or calls again. Once the window closes, you can only send pre-approved template messages ([Send messages — WhatsApp Business Platform, Meta for Developers](https://developers.facebook.com/documentation/business-messaging/whatsapp/messages/send-messages)).

Two practical consequences for a clinic:

- If the patient messaged you first, a human or an automation can reply naturally for the next 24 hours. This covers most follow-up conversations.
- If the patient only \*called\* your landline and never messaged you on WhatsApp, your first outreach has to be an approved template. Write and get those approved before you need them, not on the night you need them.

Templates must also be sent to people who have opted in to hear from you ([Send messages — WhatsApp Business Platform, Meta for Developers](https://developers.facebook.com/documentation/business-messaging/whatsapp/messages/send-messages)). In a clinic, that consent is usually collected at registration — make sure it is recorded, with a date, against the patient's number.

## Write the messages like a receptionist, not a marketer

Three messages cover most of the volume. Keep them short, specific, and in the patient's language.

\*\*The missed-call reply\*\*, sent within a minute of the missed call: "Hello, this is Sunrise Dental. Sorry we missed your call — our desk is closed until 9 AM. You can book a slot here: sunrisedental.in/book. For a medical emergency, please go to your nearest hospital or call 108." Swap in your clinic name and booking link.

\*\*The booking confirmation\*\*, sent the moment a slot is taken: "Booked: Dr Mehta, Tuesday 11:30 AM, Sunrise Dental, Baner Road. Reply RESCHEDULE if you need a different time. Please bring your previous prescriptions."

\*\*The morning callback promise\*\*, for anything the automation could not resolve: "Thank you — our team will call you after 9 AM about this. If it is urgent tonight, please go to your nearest hospital or call 108."

Notice what none of these do: they do not diagnose, they do not reassure a worried patient about symptoms, and they do not promise a doctor will call at a specific minute.

## Never automate the emergency path

This is the line that matters most, and it is worth being rigid about it.

An automated system should never assess symptoms, never tell a caller their problem can wait until morning, and never be the last thing a person in trouble hears. Every after-hours flow should carry a visible escalation instruction — go to the nearest hospital, or call 108 — and it should appear early in the message rather than buried at the end.

If your clinic offers a genuine on-call doctor line, make the automation hand off to it by name and number instead of taking a message. If it does not, say so plainly rather than implying someone is monitoring the line overnight.

Accreditation frameworks treat access and continuity of care as a core area precisely because the handover points — between shifts, between systems, between people — are where patients get lost ([National Accreditation Board for Hospitals and Healthcare Providers…](https://journals.lww.com/cmii/fulltext/2017/15030/national_accreditation_board_for_hospitals_and.15.aspx)). An after-hours system is one long handover point.

## Run the morning handover as a routine

At 9 AM someone should open a single list of everything that happened overnight, and work it top to bottom:

1. \*\*Unresolved clinical concerns first.\*\* These go to the doctor, not the receptionist.
2. \*\*Bookings the automation completed.\*\* Verify the slot is real, the doctor is available, and the patient got a confirmation.
3. \*\*Everything else\*\* — fee questions, report status, directions.

The list should be short. If it is long every morning, that is not a handover problem; it means your daytime phone coverage is the actual issue and after-hours automation is hiding it.

## Measure four things, not forty

- \*\*Calls missed per day\*\*, split by closed hours and open-but-busy. The second number is the one that usually surprises owners.
- \*\*Reply rate\*\* — of the missed calls, how many got an automated response within a minute.
- \*\*Recovery rate\*\* — of those, how many resulted in a booking within 24 hours.
- \*\*Morning queue size\*\* — items waiting for a human at 9 AM, and how long they wait.

Track these weekly for a month before you change anything else. Most clinics discover their problem is concentrated in one specific window — Saturday afternoons, or the 1 to 4 PM lull — and that one fix does more than a full automation programme.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do we need a separate phone number for after-hours calls?

No. Conditional forwarding on your existing number is usually better: patients keep dialling the number they already have, and calls only move when nobody picks up.

### Can we send an automated WhatsApp message to someone who only called us?

Only with a pre-approved template, because no customer service window is open until the patient messages or calls you on WhatsApp itself ([Send messages — WhatsApp Business Platform, Meta for Developers](https://developers.facebook.com/documentation/business-messaging/whatsapp/messages/send-messages)). Get your templates approved in advance.

### What about patients who prefer SMS or a phone call back?

Offer the choice in the first reply and record the preference against the patient. Older patients in particular often want a callback, and an automation that ignores that preference reads as impersonal.

### Should the after-hours message mention fees?

Only if your fees are fixed and public. A wrong number quoted at 10 PM becomes an argument at the desk the next morning.
