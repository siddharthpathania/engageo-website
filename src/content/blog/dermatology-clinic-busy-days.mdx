A dermatology clinic's load is not flat. Some Tuesdays the phone rings nine times an hour. Some Saturdays it rings ninety. On a heavy day your front desk is standing with three people at the counter, two callers on hold, and a WhatsApp inbox nobody has opened since eleven.

The expensive part of a heavy day is not the queue you can see. It is the calls you never hear. Nobody who dials at 12:40 PM, gets a busy tone and rings the clinic two lanes down ever tells you they tried.

Peaks are mostly predictable, and the ones that are not can still be absorbed if the desk has a plan written down before the day starts. What follows is that plan.

## Predict the peak before it arrives

Three things drive volume at a skin clinic: weather and season, your own marketing, and the calendar.

Season is real and it repeats. A multicentre study of 6,169 patients across dermatology clinics in the north, south, east and west of India found fungal infections the single most frequent group of diagnoses, at 26.1 per cent, ahead of autoimmune skin diseases at 19.4 per cent ([Distribution and Predictors of Skin Diseases in a Multicenter Clini…](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13199219/)). That same study found no significant difference between regions for fungal disease, so do not assume your city's climate alone makes your case mix unusual ([Distribution and Predictors of Skin Diseases in a Multicenter Clini…](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13199219/)). What it does flag is timing: the authors caution that their June to September collection window may over-represent fungal and parasitic presentations ([Distribution and Predictors of Skin Diseases in a Multicenter Clini…](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13199219/)), and that caveat is the operations lesson hiding in a clinical paper: what walks through your door shifts with the season.

You do not need a journal to know your own monsoon is busy. You need your own numbers so you can staff it. Pull twelve months of the appointment book and the call log and count two things: booked consultations per day, and inbound calls per hour of the day. Three patterns usually fall out. The week after every campaign. The fortnight after the first heavy rain in your city. The Monday after every long weekend and the days either side of a major festival, when nobody wants to come in and then everybody does.

Write the next quarter's heavy days on one page and put it where the rota is made. A peak that is on the calendar is a staffing decision. A peak that is not is a bad afternoon.

## Protect the phone line on the days you flagged

On a marked peak day, split the roles. One person owns the counter, one person owns the phone. The same human cannot do both at 11:30 AM on a Saturday, and asking them to means both jobs are done badly.

Then set an answer-or-text rule and make it absolute: no call ends in silence. If a call is not picked up, the caller gets a message within a minute or two that says the clinic is busy, gives a booking link, and names a window when someone will ring back. Whether that runs off a staff rota, an answering service, or a tool like Engageo on your existing number matters less than the rule itself.

Three more things worth deciding in advance:

- What "full" means. When Thursday's book is closed, the desk should say so on the first call and offer Friday, not take a hopeful walk-in and sink the schedule.
- Where the repeat questions live. Timings, consultation fee, location, parking, which days Dr Mehta sits. Put them in a WhatsApp auto-reply and on your Google Business Profile so they are never answered by voice on your worst day.
- Who answers the overflow. A second number that rings a colleague at the other branch is better than a voicemail nobody clears.

## Sort enquiries so the urgent ones do not wait behind price questions

This is operational triage, not clinical triage. The desk is sorting the type of request, never the seriousness of a skin complaint.

Six buckets cover almost everything:

1. Someone in distress or describing an emergency. The desk does not assess and does not advise. Direct them to the nearest hospital emergency department, and log the call.
2. A clinical question from a patient after a procedure. Never answered at the counter. It goes to a clinician with a same-day callback promise.
3. A new booking. Handle it now. It is revenue and it is perishable.
4. A reschedule or cancellation. Handle it now. It frees a slot you can still resell today.
5. Reports, invoices, insurance letters, forms. Batch these and promise them by end of day.
6. Fees, timings, directions, Saturday availability. Answer by text, not by voice.

Only buckets one, three and four earn the right to interrupt a busy floor. Everything else can be queued without costing the clinic anything, as long as the caller is told what will happen and when.

## Batch the callbacks instead of scattering them

Two fixed callback windows a day work better than callbacks squeezed between patients. Something like 1:30 to 2:15 PM and 7:00 to 7:45 PM. Tell every caller which window they are in.

What you write in the message matters more than clinics expect. In two randomised trials at a London NHS hospital trust, with 10,111 and 9,848 patients, an SMS reminder that stated the specific cost of a missed appointment produced a non-attendance rate of 8.4 per cent against 11.1 per cent for the trust's existing message, and beat a vaguer version mentioning costs in general, 8.2 per cent against 9.9 per cent ([Stating Appointment Costs in SMS Reminders Reduces Missed Hospital…](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4569397/)). Different health system, different economics, but the transferable lesson is that concrete beats generic in a reminder.

The channel has rules of its own, and they shape how you batch. When a patient replies to a message you sent on WhatsApp, that opens a 24-hour customer service window, and marketing templates sent inside it do not count towards the per-user marketing limit ([Per-user marketing template message limits](https://developers.facebook.com/documentation/business-messaging/whatsapp/templates/marketing-templates/per-user-limits)). That limit is set per person, against a moving view of how much marketing that person has been reading lately ([Per-user marketing template message limits](https://developers.facebook.com/documentation/business-messaging/whatsapp/templates/marketing-templates/per-user-limits)), so it is not something you can buy your way past. Outside the window, template messages are the only type you can send at all, and every template is categorised as authentication, marketing or utility ([Template fundamentals](https://developers.facebook.com/documentation/business-messaging/whatsapp/templates/overview)). Booking confirmations and appointment reminders sit in utility. Offers are marketing, and if you keep resending to someone who has already reached their limit, further attempts to that person can be blocked for up to 24 hours and return error 131049 ([Per-user marketing template message limits](https://developers.facebook.com/documentation/business-messaging/whatsapp/templates/marketing-templates/per-user-limits)). Template quality is rated on usage, customer feedback and engagement ([Template fundamentals](https://developers.facebook.com/documentation/business-messaging/whatsapp/templates/overview)), so a clinic that blasts discount offers quietly degrades the same channel it relies on for reminders.

## Automate in this order, and stop where judgement starts

1. The missed-call text-back. It is the one that works hardest on exactly the days you are worst at answering, and it asks least of the desk.
2. Confirmation at the moment of booking, a reminder the day before, a reminder on the morning.
3. A short auto-reply covering fees, timings and location.
4. A callback queue that captures name, number, bucket and promised window.
5. Campaign follow-up, staggered rather than blasted.

Leave these alone. Clinical answers. Complaints. Results. The first call from a patient who is upset. Any conversation about money that is not a published price. Automation is for the part of the conversation that is identical every time, and a dermatology practice keeps its personal feel by spending the hours it saves on the calls that are not.

## Stop manufacturing your own peaks

Campaign spikes are self-inflicted, which makes them the easiest kind to fix. Land offers on a Tuesday or Wednesday, not Friday evening. Stagger a send across three days instead of one. Decide beforehand how many new campaign bookings the clinic can absorb per day and cap intake there.

Before any of that, check you are allowed to send at all. To push commercial communication over Indian telecom channels you must register as a principal entity, send through a registered header of at most eleven alphanumeric characters, register your content template structures, and obtain consent through a registered consent template; miss a step and you simply cannot send ([Advice to Senders, Telecom Regulatory Authority of India](https://trai.gov.in/advice-to-senders)).

## Frequently asked questions

### How far ahead can I actually predict a busy day?

Campaign peaks, to the day, because you choose them. Festival and long-weekend peaks, to the week, from the calendar. Weather-driven peaks, roughly, and only once you have a year of your own booking data to compare this season against.

### Should the front desk ever answer a clinical question on the phone?

No. Take the number, tell the patient when a clinician will ring back, and keep that promise. A desk that guesses once will guess again on a busier day.

### Is an automated text rude when someone wanted a person?

Less rude than a busy tone. A message inside two minutes that names a callback window is a kept promise. The failure mode to avoid is the message that promises a callback nobody makes.

### We are a two-doctor clinic. Is any of this overkill?

Start with two things only: the missed-call text-back and the day-before reminder. Add the callback queue when the desk starts losing names on paper.
