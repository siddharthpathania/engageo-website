The question usually arrives as a salary question. Your receptionist is drowning, the phone rings while a patient stands at the desk waiting to pay, and somebody suggests putting one more person on the front. Six months later you have two people at the desk and the phone still rings out at 7:30 PM.

The hire may well be the right answer. You cannot know until you have measured what the desk is actually carrying, and most clinics never have. Acting on a feeling means a salary you pay every month for years, plus the statutory load on top.

This is the measurement, the two cost structures, what software genuinely cannot do, and a checklist you can run this week.

## Measure the load before you price the fix

The front desk is not one job. It is at least five stacked on one chair: answering the phone, handling walk-ins, booking and rescheduling, billing and paperwork, and chasing reports. Each peaks at a different time and fails differently when overloaded.

It is also more judgement-heavy than the job title suggests. A systematic review of 29 UK studies of general practice receptionists found that routine booking took on the character of triage whenever demand outstripped slots, with reception staff effectively deciding clinical urgency; the same staff communicated test results, including abnormal ones, and handled repeat prescriptions, largely without recognition, support or training ([Impact of the clinically oriented roles of a general practice recep…](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11849695/)). Those studies are British, so read them as a description of the role rather than a measurement of your clinic; the pattern will still be recognisable at your desk.

### The two-week count

Six numbers. An hourly paper tally at the desk is enough if your phone system will not export logs.

- Calls offered and calls answered, by hour, by day.
- Missed calls by hour. Look for the shape: a 9 to 10 AM IST spike, a lunch hole, or a flat bleed after the desk goes home.
- Walk-ins per hour.
- Minutes per billing interaction. Time ten of them with a stopwatch rather than guessing.
- Reports chased per day.
- Interruptions: how many times the person at the desk abandons a task half-finished.

Two weeks is the minimum because one week lies; a festival, a camp or one doctor on leave will distort it. What you end up with is not a number but a shape, and the shape decides everything. Pressure spread evenly across a working day is a different problem from pressure concentrated in two hours, which is different again from pressure that sits entirely outside your working hours.

## What a second hire actually costs

The salary is the visible part and the smallest part of the surprise. Four other costs are real and none of them appear on the offer letter.

Statutory contributions come first. For covered employees the employer pays 3.25% of wages towards ESI, on top of the 0.75% deducted from the employee, at rates in force since 1 July 2019, and the money must reach ESIC within 15 days of the end of the month in which it falls due ([ESIC Contribution | Employees' State Insurance Corporation](https://esic.gov.in/contribution)). Employees on a daily average wage up to 176 rupees are exempt from their own share, but the employer's share is still payable ([ESIC Contribution | Employees' State Insurance Corporation](https://esic.gov.in/contribution)). Provident fund sits above that. Budget the loaded cost, not the take-home.

Then the ramp. A new person at a medical front desk is not useful on day one. They have to learn which cases each doctor will squeeze in today, which insurer wants which form, and how to say no to a walk-in without losing them. That takes weeks, and during those weeks your existing receptionist is slower, not faster, because she is teaching.

Then coverage. One person cannot hold 8 AM to 9 PM. Two people covering a thirteen-hour day do not give you thirteen hours of two people; they give you a handover.

Then supervision and attrition. Someone has to check the new person's work, and that someone is usually you. Every exit makes you pay the ramp again. And an extra pair of hands is not automatically a trained pair: the review found the clinically adjacent parts of the job rarely came with training ([Impact of the clinically oriented roles of a general practice recep…](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11849695/)), so a second hire does not by itself fix a judgement problem.

None of this makes hiring wrong. It makes hiring expensive in a way the salary line does not show, which is why it should answer a measured problem rather than a bad week.

## What automation genuinely does well

Automation earns its place on work that is repetitive, identical every time, high in volume and often outside working hours. Reminders are the clearest case, and the one place with real evidence rather than vendor enthusiasm.

A Cochrane systematic review of eight randomised controlled trials covering 6,615 participants found that mobile phone text message reminders increased attendance at scheduled healthcare appointments compared with no reminder: attendance of 78.6% with reminders against 67.8% without, a risk ratio of 1.14 with a 95% confidence interval of 1.03 to 1.26 across seven trials and 5,841 participants, at moderate quality of evidence ([Mobile phone messaging reminders for attendance at healthcare appoi…](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD007458_mobile-phone-messaging-reminders-attendance-healthcare-appointments)). Two of the included studies found cost per attendance was 55% and 65% lower than phone call reminders ([Mobile phone messaging reminders for attendance at healthcare appoi…](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD007458_mobile-phone-messaging-reminders-attendance-healthcare-appointments)).

The most useful finding is the least quoted one. Text reminders performed about the same as staff telephoning patients, a risk ratio of 0.99 with a confidence interval of 0.95 to 1.02 ([Mobile phone messaging reminders for attendance at healthcare appoi…](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD007458_mobile-phone-messaging-reminders-attendance-healthcare-appointments)). So you are not choosing between a good reminder and a worse one. You are choosing between the same outcome paid for in staff hours and paid for in messages.

Be honest about what that evidence covers: text reminders, in trial conditions, measured on attendance. It is not evidence that software can run a front desk. The same logic extends sensibly to after-hours answering, a WhatsApp text-back with a booking link when a call is missed, confirmations and six-month recalls. ## What automation cannot do

Four things, and you should assume all four.

- Judgement about urgency. Deciding who gets squeezed in today is a clinical decision wearing an administrative hat ([Impact of the clinically oriented roles of a general practice recep…](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11849695/)). Keep a human in that loop and give that human a written protocol.
- Distress. A frightened caller, a bereaved family, a patient arguing about a bill. Each needs a person who has time, which is the argument for automating the routine volume around them.
- Emergencies. Every automated path must end the same way when someone describes a medical emergency: tell them to go to the nearest hospital or emergency service immediately. No script, and no receptionist, should be doing anything else with that call.
- Messaging people who have not agreed to it. WhatsApp's business policy states you may only contact people on WhatsApp if they have given you their mobile number and you have received opt-in permission confirming they wish to receive subsequent messages or calls from you ([Business Policy | WhatsApp for Business](https://whatsappbusiness.com/policy/)). It also allows automation in the 24-hour customer service window after a patient messages you, but requires prompt, clear and direct escalation paths to a human alongside it, and requires you to respect any request to stop ([Business Policy | WhatsApp for Business](https://whatsappbusiness.com/policy/)). Automation does not remove the human. It changes when the human is needed.

## A decision checklist

Run this against your two weeks of numbers.

1. Missed calls cluster outside working hours. Automate first. A second salary buys you nothing at 9:42 PM.
2. Missed calls cluster in two predictable peaks inside working hours. Automate the overflow, re-measure for two weeks, then decide on the hire with better numbers.
3. Missed calls are spread evenly across the day and your answered rate is still poor. That is a headcount problem. Hire.
4. The bottleneck is billing, paperwork or report chasing rather than calls. Hire, or restructure the role. That is document and judgement work.
5. Your desk makes urgency decisions with no written protocol, which the review found is common and usually unsupported ([Impact of the clinically oriented roles of a general practice recep…](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11849695/)). Neither a hire nor software fixes that on its own. Write the protocol first.
6. You cannot answer points 1 to 5. Do the count. You are guessing.
7. Whatever you choose, re-measure the same six numbers 30 days later. If they have not moved, you solved the wrong problem.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should I measure before I decide?

Two full weeks, chosen to look like a normal month rather than a festival week or a leave week. If your patient mix swings seasonally, repeat the count in the other season before committing to a permanent salary.

### Will patients accept an automated reply instead of a person?

Compliance comes before preference. On WhatsApp you need opt-in permission before you message someone, and a clear route to a human when they want one ([Business Policy | WhatsApp for Business](https://whatsappbusiness.com/policy/)). Once that is in place, measure it the way you measured the load: how many automated conversations end in a booking, and how many escalate.

### Can automation replace my receptionist entirely?

No. Parts of the job that look administrative from outside include urgency judgement, results communication and prescription handling, and those carry real consequences ([Impact of the clinically oriented roles of a general practice recep…](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11849695/)). The realistic goal is to take repetitive volume off the desk so the person there has time for the work that needs a person.

### What if I hire and automate in the same month?

You can, and sometimes cash flow forces it. You just lose the ability to tell which one worked. If you must do both, stagger them by 30 days and keep the six numbers running throughout.
